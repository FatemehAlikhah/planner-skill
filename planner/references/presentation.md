# Presentation Guide: Outcomes, Lists and Layout

Read this file before writing any plan, review or summary the user will read. A plan people can scan in ten seconds gets followed; a plan that reads like a wall of text gets closed. The goal of every choice below is the same: help the reader see (1) what to do, (2) why it is worth it, and (3) how they will know it worked.

## 1. Writing the Expected Outcomes block

The block is the persuasive heart of a plan. It answers "what do I get?" in a way that is specific enough to check and honest enough to trust. Build it in three layers:

**Headline (one sentence).** The single most valuable change this plan produces, written as a result and not as an activity.
- Weak: "You will work on the case study this week."
- Strong: "By Friday the case study is sent for review and no longer sits in your head."

**Outcomes (2 to 4 rows).** Each row has three parts, because a result without a signal cannot be checked and a signal without meaning does not motivate:

| What changes | Why it matters | How you will know |
|---|---|---|
| Case-study draft finished | Unblocks the portfolio update | File sent, review requested |
| Workouts back in rhythm | Protects energy for deep work | Tue and Thu both done |

**Two tiers, stated honestly.**
- **Core** (if the main priorities are done): what the plan reliably produces.
- **Stretch** (if capacity allows): what extra becomes possible. Omit when it would be invented.

Rules that keep this persuasive without becoming hype:
- Tie every outcome to a real priority in the plan. If an outcome cannot be traced to a task, delete it.
- Prefer concrete nouns and verbs ("sent", "published", "decided", "cleared") over feelings ("feel better", "more productive").
- Show the cost side once, briefly, when it helps trust: what this plan deliberately leaves out and why ("Newsletter moved to next week to protect the case study").
- Never use guilt or fear framing. Describe what the plan makes possible.
- Tone for analytical readers: reasoning first, encouragement only as a by-product of clarity. Skip slogans and motivational filler.

## 2. Choosing the right shape for each kind of content

Match the shape to how the reader will use the content. Mixed shapes in one plan are normal; the same shape everywhere is what makes plans feel flat.

| Content | Best shape | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1 to 3 priorities | Numbered list, each with a one-line "why" | Order matters and the reason keeps them honest |
| Tasks for a day | Checklist with time estimate at the end | Reader ticks them off, estimates expose overload |
| Week or month distribution | Table: rows = days or weeks, columns = focus, tasks, load | Comparison across time is the point |
| Capacity vs. plan | Small bar or ratio ("6h planned / 8h available") | Overload is visible without explanation |
| Habits | Grid or single line per habit with streak | Pattern recognition |
| Goals with milestones | Goal as heading, milestones as short nested list with dates | Shows the path, not only the destination |
| Trade-offs and risks | Two-column "Risk / Mitigation" table | Pairs the problem with its answer |
| Reviews | Three short blocks: What happened, Pattern, Adjustment | Ends with one decision, not a recap |
| Anything with more than 7 items | Group under labeled sub-headings | Working memory holds about 5 to 7 items |

Avoid: bullet lists of full paragraphs, nested lists deeper than two levels, tables with more than 5 columns, and lists where every item has equal visual weight (mark the one that matters most).

## 3. Layout and hierarchy rules

1. **Lead with the answer.** The first screen shows the headline outcome and the top priorities. Details follow.
2. **One idea per block.** A block is a heading plus at most a short table or list. Separate blocks with a heading or a horizontal rule, not extra prose.
3. **Three levels of emphasis at most.** Heading, bold key phrase, normal text. Use bold for the one phrase per item the eye should catch, not for whole sentences.
4. **Consistent item anatomy.** If the first task reads "Draft case study (90 min)", every task follows the same pattern. Consistency lets the eye skim.
5. **Whitespace is a feature.** Keep blocks short; one blank line between blocks.
6. **Markers with meaning.** If using symbols, give each a single job (for example ✅ done, 🔸 priority, ⏱ time, 🎯 outcome) and use at most four types. Decorative emoji lower the credibility of an analytical plan, so skip them when the user's style is sober.
7. **Order by decision value.** Priorities, then schedule, then risks, then outcomes. The reader can stop at any point and still have the most important part.
8. **Close with one next action.** A plan ends with the first concrete step, so starting costs nothing.

Skeleton for a weekly plan:

```
## [Period] at a glance
🎯 Headline outcome (one sentence)

### Priorities
1. **Priority** : why it matters
2. ...

### Distribution
| Day | Focus | Tasks | Load |
|---|---|---|---|

### Risks and protections
| Risk | What the plan does about it |
|---|---|

### Expected Outcomes
Headline, outcome table, core vs stretch.

### First step
One concrete action for today.
```

## 4. Persian (RTL) and bilingual details

- Write headings and body fully in Persian when the user writes Persian; keep technical terms or product names in their original script inside the sentence.
- In tables, put the label column first (it sits on the right in RTL). Keep numbers consistent: use the numeral style the user uses (Persian or Latin digits) throughout.
- Jalali dates: show weekday and date together (شنبه ۲۵ مهر) so the week boundary is obvious. A Jalali week starts on Saturday.
- Avoid mixing LTR fragments mid-line in lists where punctuation can jump sides; move English terms into parentheses at the end of the phrase.
- Do not translate a block word for word from English templates. Rephrase so it reads naturally; "Expected Outcomes" becomes something like «دستاوردهای مورد انتظار» or «اگر این برنامه را دنبال کنی، چه به دست می‌آوری».

## 5. When to render a visual artifact instead of chat text

Chat text is right for a plan the user reads once. Offer a rendered page (an HTML or document artifact) when the plan will be kept, revisited, shared or printed, or when it contains a capacity chart, habit grid or multi-week table that benefits from real layout. Offer in one line; do not build unasked for quick plans. In a rendered version:

- Put the headline outcome in a prominent top card, then priorities, then the schedule table.
- Show capacity as a simple progress bar and habits as a small grid.
- Use one accent color for "what matters" and neutral tones for everything else.
- Support RTL direction and a Persian-friendly font when the content is Persian.

## 6. Before and after example

**Before** (hard to scan, outcome is generic):

> This week you should work on your case study, exercise on Tuesday and Thursday, and also prepare the newsletter and reply to emails. If you do all this you will be more productive and feel better.

**After:**

> ### این هفته در یک نگاه
> 🎯 تا جمعه، پیش‌نویس کیس‌استادی برای بازبینی ارسال شده و دیگر ذهنت را درگیر نمی‌کند.
>
> **اولویت‌ها**
> 1. **پیش‌نویس کیس‌استادی** (۳ جلسه × ۹۰ دقیقه): مسیر بروزرسانی پورتفولیو را باز می‌کند
> 2. **ورزش سه‌شنبه و پنجشنبه**: انرژی لازم برای کار عمیق را حفظ می‌کند
>
> | دستاورد | چرا مهم است | نشانه تحقق |
> |---|---|---|
> | پیش‌نویس نهایی | قبل از بروزرسانی پورتفولیو لازم است | فایل ارسال و بازبینی درخواست شده |
> | ریتم ورزش | حفظ انرژی | هر دو جلسه انجام شده |
>
> **عمداً بیرون ماند:** خبرنامه، به هفته بعد منتقل شد تا اولویت اول فشرده نشود.
